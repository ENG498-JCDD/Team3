# Exploring the Data Design of our Project on SNAP Benefits 

This page covers our project's data design - the types of data we found and chose to use, the concepts they reflect from our class readings on social justice within TPC, the benefits and limitations of our data (as well as how these limitations could be addressed with additional data), and the next steps we envision the field of TPC to take in furthering the research we've done through this project.  

## Assessing the Data We Used

There were three main types of Wake County data our team looked for when beginning this project: SNAP enrollment data, SNAP recipient demographic data, and food insecurity data. We also looked for information on the program's eligibility requirements. We mainly utilized data from various 2023 Wake County Census tables, as these tables provided us with detailed breakdowns of the total amount of SNAP recipients in the county and the various demographics they represent (age, disability status, income level, number of children, etc). We also ended up investigating NC ACS SNAP data to examine the race demographics of SNAP in the county, as the Census data didn't include a breakdown of race. Additionally, we considered data from [Mind the Meal Gap](https://map.feedingamerica.org/), and gathered helpful stats on SNAP eligibility and Wake County's food insecurity levels from sources like the [USDA's Food and Nutrition Service](https://www.fns.usda.gov/snap/recipient/eligibility), the [Wake County Food Security Program](https://www.wake.gov/departments-government/wake-county-food-security-program), and the [Food Bank of Central & Eastern North Carolina](https://foodbankcenc.org/hunger-impact/county/wake). Our data was predominantly quantitative, mainly showcasing exact numbers for the county's SNAP recipient pool and breaking these numbers down based on demographics and/or food insecurity levels. 

## Applying Concepts from the Readings

There are several concepts from the class readings that can be applied to our findings to consider what our data does and doesn't feature, including the structuralist perspective, collectivization, and materialization. 

### The Structuralist Perspective 

The structuralist perspective focuses on how TPC can create or change social structures to improve the conditions of marginalized individuals. The data we collected on Wake County SNAP recipient demographics and the stats we gathered on the county's food insecurity levels reveal a structural issue: SNAP's eligibility requirements automatically disqualify 51% of the county's food insecure population from even applying for the program, blocking them from a major source of food support. One of our proposed solutions for addressing the SNAP issue includes implementing structural changes by adjusting SNAP's eligibility requirements so that more food insecure people can apply for the program and receive the support they need. However, it's important to note that our data only examines Wake County, and that further research on other counties and more states could help solidify our point and showcase how many food insecure people are being excluded from the program. 

### Collectivization 

Collectivization involves connecting marginalized users with relevant support networks and resources. This concept is baked into our project via our use of food bank data. A major source of information for us was the Food Bank of Central & Eastern North Carolina, who have collected stats on Wake County's food insecurity levels and are working to fill the gap in support for food insecure residents who don't qualify for SNAP or who need additional support. One potential solution that arises from our findings is for more resources to be offered as a substitute to SNAP, such as by providing more funding and donations to the county's food banks or running outreach programs to bring food to those who can't access it on their own. Notably, one feature missing from our project is data on how many food insecure Wake County citizens utilize non-SNAP programs; having this data could help us understand how much these programs are being utilized and identify any issues with scope or access. 

### Materialization

The concept of materialization refers to the material conditions that create oppressive environments for marginalized groups. A key part of the issue our project investigates is the material condition of lack of food support, which is creating an oppressive environment of food insecurity for over 126,000 county residents; SNAP benefits are also arguably a material condition here, as the lack of access to them is contributing to this oppressive environment and perpetuating the county's food insecurity levels. In particular, our stats on food insecurity and our NC ACS SNAP data found that these conditions are especially oppressive to groups like Black people and single-parent households. Our proposed solutions for addressing the issue involve bridging this gap by providing these groups with positive material conditions (aka food and/or financial support) for reducing their food insecurity. While our quantitative data adequately illustrates the materialization concept, it could be improved upon with qualitative data, such as interviews with these groups about their personal experiences and the specific oppressive conditions created by their food insecurity. 

## Weighing Our Data's Benefits and Limitations

Our 2023 Census data provided several benefits; it offered information specific to Wake County and its various townships, it included breakdowns of several key demographics we wanted to examine, and it provided a comparison between the number of SNAP participants and non-SNAP participants for each demographic. The use of this data allowed our project to narrow its scope down to just the county, provided us with a way to view how many people from each of our key demographics were on SNAP, and gave us a way to compare the number of SNAP vs non-SNAP users. For our problem scenario about a local policymaker, these features would be beneficial in that they would give the policymaker a way to quickly view relevant, county-specific insights on a wide variety of demographics and with an accurate match-up of the number of SNAP and non-SNAP users in Wake County. The main limitation of the 2023 Census data was that its table on Wake County SNAP recipients by race only provided data on White households, making it unusable for this project; our NC ACS data was beneficial here in that it provided some data on SNAP users that was sorted by race. However, this data was _also_ limited due to the fact that it didn't have a category for Hispanic/Latino citizens despite that group being one of the most affected by food insecurity in the county. 

Although our project sufficiently answered many of the questions we had and helped us address our project aims, there were a few issues we looked into where further research/or and additional data will be needed. Data tracking Wake County's overall and demographic food insecurity levels over the years would be beneficial, as comparing it to the data tracking SNAP enrollment levels over time could potentially provide insights about SNAP's impact on food insecurity. Additionally, qualitative data on the conditions of food insecure groups and the resources they use aside from SNAP could be key to gaining a greater understanding of the county's food insecure population and the factors that are positively or negatively impacting their access to food. Lastly, sufficient county data on SNAP use by race is urgently needed to address the extreme food insecurity levels Black people face in Wake County, as the limited race data available made it difficult to see what percentage of this group are receiving the supplemental support they need. 

## Considering Potential Next Steps for TPC 

TPC's potential next steps could include expanding upon Wake County's existing SNAP-use-by-race data to paint a clearer picture of how the needs of food insecure Black citizens are being met, as well as locating data that tracks the county's food insecurity levels over time, which could be directly compared with the level of SNAP enrollment over time to examine SNAP's potential impacts on food insecurity. It's also important for future research to be done on the specific, primary reasons people in Wake County are ineligible for (or being denied for) SNAP, as this could help identify some of the barriers that must be overcome in order for Wake County's food insecure population to receive the support it needs.
